Seven letters name the white keys. The five black keys in each octave get their names from the white keys beside them, using two signs:

- A **sharp**, written ♯, raises a note by one half step.
- A **flat**, written ♭, lowers a note by one half step.

So the black key just above C is **C♯** ("C sharp"). Click the keys around the marked note below and watch its name change.

## One key, two names

That same black key is also just below D, so it can equally be called **D♭** ("D flat"). Both names are correct, and they sound identical. Play the black keys below and read the two names on each.

Two names for one pitch are called **enharmonic**. The five pairs are:

- C♯ and D♭
- D♯ and E♭
- F♯ and G♭
- G♯ and A♭
- A♯ and B♭

> A sharp means a half step up. A flat means a half step down. Each black key has a sharp name and a flat name.

Which name to use depends on the music the note is in, and Level 3 explains how the choice is made. Until then, treat the two names as interchangeable.

## Where the black keys are missing

There is no black key between E and F, or between B and C, because those notes are already a half step apart. Sharps and flats still mean what they always mean, so a half step above E is simply F. You will occasionally see F written as E♯, or B written as C♭, but that is rare, and you can leave it until it comes up.

## Not only on the piano

Sharps and flats are not a piano idea. On a guitar there are no black and white keys at all: every fret is one half step, and F♯ is just the fret after F. The widget at the top of this lesson shows the same note in both places.
