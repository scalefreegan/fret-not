The octave blends because its ratio, 2 to 1, is as simple as a ratio can be. That idea goes further. Whether any two notes sound smooth or rough together depends largely on how simple the ratio between their frequencies is.

Step through the ratios below. For each one, listen to the two notes together, look at whether the combined wave settles into a short repeating pattern, and count how many harmonics on the two ladders line up.

With 3:2, the upper note vibrates three times for every two of the lower. The combined wave repeats quickly, and every second harmonic of the upper note sits exactly on a harmonic of the lower. With 16:15 the two notes are close together, almost nothing lines up, and several harmonics sit right next to each other without meeting.

## Smooth and rough

Two frequencies that nearly coincide interfere with each other. They drift into step and out again many times a second, and you hear that as a pulsing or buzzing roughness called **beating**. Notes whose harmonics mostly coincide have very little of it. Notes whose harmonics mostly just miss have a lot.

Musicians call a smooth, settled combination **consonant** and a rough, tense one **dissonant**.

> Simple frequency ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3) give consonance because the notes share harmonics. Complicated ratios give dissonance because their harmonics clash.

## Three sounds worth knowing by name

The exercises below ask you to tell apart three of the combinations you just heard, using the names musicians give them:

- the **octave**, 2:1, the most blended of all
- the **perfect fifth**, 3:2, open and strong
- the **minor second**, about 16:15, two neighbouring notes clashing

These names are explained properly in Level 3. For now they are just labels for three very different sounds.

## Neither is better

Dissonance is not wrong. Music moves by building tension and letting it go, and dissonance is the tension. What matters is knowing which sound you are choosing.

One more thing before Level 1. The harmonics inside a single note already contain these ratios: harmonics 2 and 3 are a perfect fifth apart, and harmonics 3 and 4 are 4:3 apart. The notes that sound good together are, in a real sense, the ones already hiding inside each other.
