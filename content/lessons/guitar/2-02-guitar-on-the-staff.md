Tab tells you where to put your fingers. The staff tells you which notes you are playing, and it is the shared language of all instruments. Guitarists who can read both can learn from anything.

Guitar music uses the **treble clef** only. But there is a twist.

## Written an octave higher than it sounds

The guitar is lower than it seems. Its lowest string is the E far below middle C, and even its highest open string is only the E just above middle C. Written at their true pitch, most guitar notes would dangle on stacks of ledger lines below the treble staff.

So, by long-standing agreement, **guitar music is written one octave higher than it sounds**. Nothing changes about how you read: the lines are still E G B D F and the spaces F A C E. It is simply understood that what comes out of the instrument is an octave lower.

> Guitar is written in the treble clef, an octave above its real sound. Read it like any treble staff.

## The open strings on the staff

Play this slowly. It is the six open strings from lowest to highest, with the staff above showing how each is written.

```widget
type: tab
title: The six open strings
notes: "6/0:h 5/0:h | 4/0:h 3/0:h | 2/0:h 1/0:h"
tempo: 70
```

Where they sit, from string 6 up to string 1:

- **String 6, E**: hanging under the third ledger line below the staff.
- **String 5, A**: on the second ledger line below the staff.
- **String 4, D**: in the space just below the bottom line.
- **String 3, G**: on line 2, the clef's own line.
- **String 2, B**: on the middle line.
- **String 1, E**: in the top space.

Strings 3, 2 and 1 land on three easy landmarks: the G line, the middle line and the top space.

## One written note, several places

On a piano each written note is one key. On a guitar a written note can often be played in more than one place, as you saw in Level 1. The staff does not say which. Tab does. That is why the two are so often printed together.

Follow this phrase on both. It stays on strings 1 and 2 near the nut.

title: Ode to Joy (Beethoven, Symphony No. 9), first phrase
